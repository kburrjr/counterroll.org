Read the Introduction

The book before this one left a congregation at the mount with the code delivered and the dwelling raised in the midst of the tents. This one takes up the same people a month later and does what a code requires of any people who mean to live under it: it counts them. Leviticus fixed what they were to do before the LORD; Numbers fixes who they are, where every house stands, and by what warrant the host moves. The ledger opens on the first day of the second month in the wilderness of Sinai, and the administration of the covenant begins with it.

Volume I of Numbers runs from the first chapter through the tenth, and its argument is the ordering of a host. The command that opens the record is a command to take the sum of all the congregation, after their families, by the house of their fathers, with the number of their names, and the answer to it is the register of the host, tribe by tribe, Levi alone reserved out of the count and enrolled to another service. On that register the camp is drawn, three tribes to a side about the dwelling, every man under his own standard, the tabernacle at the centre. The Levites are given the inventory of the sanctuary, every board and vessel named with the hand that carries it. The camp is cleansed, restitution made with the fifth part added, and the hidden thing tried by the water of jealousy and the written curse. A Nazarite may bind himself by vow, its terms stated and its offerings fixed. Twelve princes bring identical gifts on twelve days, and the dedication of the altar is entered name by name. The lamps are lit, the Levites installed with their term of service fixed, and the Passover kept in the wilderness with provision in the second month for the man whom a dead body or a journey has deferred. The cloud rests upon the dwelling, the silver trumpets sound the assembly and the alarm and the feasts, and the host departs out of the wilderness of Sinai.

That departure is where this volume closes, and the close is not an arbitrary cut. The first ten chapters hold the whole of the Sinai ordering: the numbering, the camp, the charges of the ministers, the cleansing, the vows, the register of the gifts, the lamps, the trumpets, and the movement orders that set the camp in motion. The eleventh chapter is the turn of another thing, the murmuring of a host on the march, and the wilderness administration that follows it, with the reconnaissance of the land, the refusal at the border, and the sentence measured a year for a day, is carried in the second volume. The tenth chapter ends with the ark going before the host three days' journey to search out a resting place, and it closes over the encampment at rest; the order is complete and the people are moving, which is exactly the estate a book about an ordered host should leave them in.

The doctrine carried through these chapters is the law of the ledger itself, and it is the plain sense of the record rather than a theory laid over it. A people becomes administrable when it is counted. The census is a constitutional register, for a name that is not on the roll has no standing to serve, no standing to receive, and no place among the companies of the camp. The camp-order is jurisdiction drawn upon the ground, the law at its centre and an assigned place for every house. The inventory of the dwelling is the ledger of the sovereign venue, itemized to the keeper who answers for it, and the cleansing ordinance keeps the boundary of that venue pure. The vow is a voluntary obligation with its terms and its discharge; the gifts of the princes are a register of equal tenders; the lamps and the trumpets are the signals of the house and of the field. Over every entry stands one formula, that it was done as the LORD commanded Moses, for an act of administration without warrant is no administration at all.

What makes this volume worth a book of its own is that the numbering is the precondition of everything the record afterward calls the people. A host that cannot be counted cannot be marshaled, provisioned, judged, or moved, and a congregation without a roll has no way of knowing who belongs to it or who answers for it. The ledger is therefore not the dull part of the record but the beginning of the standing of the nation, and the camp drawn about the dwelling is the shape of the jurisdiction that the law will govern for the rest of the wilderness and afterward in the land.

And it came to pass, when the ark set forward, that Moses said, Rise up, LORD, and let thine enemies be scattered; and let them that hate thee flee before thee. And when it rested, he said, Return, O LORD, unto the many thousands of Israel.

Numbers 10:35-36

Nothing in this volume asks for the assent of any man's unexamined habit. The scripture is quoted whole, the doctrine is named as doctrine wherever it goes past the text, and the weighing of the account belongs to whoever holds the book.
