Read the Introduction

The proverbs of Solomon the son of David, king of Israel; to know wisdom and instruction; to perceive the words of understanding; to receive the instruction of wisdom, justice, and judgment, and equity. A wise man will hear, and will increase learning; and a man of understanding shall attain unto wise counsels: to understand a proverb, and the interpretation; the words of the wise, and their dark sayings. The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge: but fools despise wisdom and instruction. Proverbs 1:1-7

Every body of law announces at its head who may speak, and by what authority the words that follow are to be received. A statute names the assembly that enacted it. A judgment names the court that entered it. A covenant names the parties who bound themselves. Proverbs does something older than any of these. It names a father, a mother, and a son, and then it sets the fear of the LORD above all three as the only ground on which anything inside the book can be known.

That order is the book's jurisdiction, and this Codex takes it as the jurisdiction of the whole. There is no rule in Proverbs that stands without the fear as its foundation, because the book does not treat wisdom as a technique to be acquired but as a way of walking before the One who sees. The Hebrew word at the head of the book is chokmah, and it is not a word for cleverness. It is the word the craftsmen are given when they cut the boards of the tabernacle to the measure they were shown on the mount. It is skill, the trained hand, the thing a man has because he has submitted to a pattern not of his own making.

Beside it stands musar, instruction, and the word carries the rod inside it. Musar is what a father gives a son who will not hear, and the book is honest about the cost of refusing it. What Proverbs offers is not advice in the modern sense, where a man may take it or leave it and nothing follows. It is the common law of the covenant, stated in single lines, and a line of it is a rule of decision. The judge who has to settle a dispute about a false witness, a struck hand, a dishonest scale, or a son who will not be governed will find the rule in this book, and will find it older than anything he is likely to be reading in its place.

The book is called the Proverbs Codex because of the word it is built on. The Hebrew mashal means both a proverb and to rule, to govern, to have dominion. The same consonants carry the saying and the authority. A proverb is not a decoration on the law. It is a rule compressed to the size of one breath, made short so that it can be remembered by men who cannot carry books, and made memorable so that it can be applied in the gate where the case is heard. Where the maxim is the Latin short form in which equity keeps its reasoning, the mashal is the Hebrew short form in which the covenant keeps its law, and the two do the same work.

This Codex sets the one beside the other. Each chapter opens with the proverb in full, in the statutory register, quoted whole and unbroken, and then works the line as what it is: a rule, with a party who relies on it and a court that must apply it. The Book of Maxims states the twelve maxims and seats them to the offices of the old court. The Psalms Codex sets the rules of procedure beside the songs. This book is the substantive law those two presuppose. It is the body of the covenant's common law, sorted and read as law.

The nine hundred and fifteen verses of Proverbs sort under seven Parts, drawn from the material itself and not forced upon it. Each Part opens into three or four chapters, twenty-five in all.

Part I, The Jurisdiction. The fear of the LORD as the ground of the whole code, from the long prologue of the first nine chapters: the father's charge, wisdom crying in the streets, the two houses and the two women, and the plain statement that the beginning of knowledge is the fear of the LORD.

Part II, The Two Ways. The choice of law. Every dispute resolves in the end to the question of which law the party walked under. The way of the righteous and the way of the wicked are set side by side from the tenth chapter onward, and the two-ways frame is the same frame Psalm 1 opens the civil Codex with and Deuteronomy 30 sets before the people as life and death.

Part III, The Tongue. Words as legal acts: the faithful witness, the false witness, the talebearer, the soft answer, the lying tongue. Defamation and the rule of evidence are here, before any statute touches them, and the false witness is treated as a fraud upon the court.

Part IV, Money, Debt, and Surety. The law of obligations. Striking hands as surety, the borrower made servant to the lender, the dishonest scale, honest weights, and the hand of the diligent. The pledge line runs from the handshake of the sixth chapter to the collateral of Article 9, and the mortgage is a man becoming surety for what he has been given.

Part V, The Household. The smallest jurisdiction, and the first one. The father as its administrator, the discipline of children as its process, the house built by wisdom, the contentious wife, and the inheritance of the fathers.

Part VI, The King and the Court. Official accountability. The throne established by righteousness, the king who judges the poor, the favor and the wrath of the crown, and the sayings of Agur against the generation that devours. Here constitutional law is stated: the ruler stands under the same maxims as the man he rules, and the officers' standard is applied to the throne itself.

Part VII, The Woman of Valor. The book does not end in instruction. It ends in a person. The acrostic of the thirty-first chapter, aleph to tav, is the whole book's wisdom done rather than heard: an estate administered, a field purchased, provision made for a household, and honor given at the gate. Eshet chayil, the woman of valor, carries the strength of an army in her name, and she is the model fiduciary of the covenant.

Every chapter opens with its proverb in full. Read the verse first, and let it stand as the statute it is. Then read the mashal beside the maxim, and see that the rule has a Latin short form and a Hebrew short form and that they say the same thing. Then read the law expanded, with the Hebrew word work done plainly, so that a term of art is not left standing in an English sentence as though the reader already owned it.

The words that recur are the ones that matter. Yirat YHWH, the fear of the LORD, is the jurisdiction. Chokmah is the skill. Musar is the instruction that carries the rod. Sedeq and mishpat are righteousness and judgment, the two words that together mean a settled order. Hesed is covenant loyalty, the obligation that outlasts convenience. Arab is to pledge, the hand struck when a man becomes surety for another. Learn the words and the rules behind them come with them.

At the end of the book a woman is praised in the gate, and the praise is measured: she bought a field, she planted a vineyard, she girded her loins, she stretched out her hand to the poor, her household was clothed, her husband was known among the elders, and her works followed her into the gate where the record is kept. That is what the whole book was for. The law of the covenant is not given to make men admire wisdom. It is given to make them administer what they have been trusted with. The Codex is the common law that says how.
