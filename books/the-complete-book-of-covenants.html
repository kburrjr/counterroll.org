Read the Introduction

The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church is the oldest continuous Christian body in Africa, and it holds what no other church holds: the whole counsel of the canon, eighty-eight books in the full count, eighty-one when certain books that the scribes joined are counted as one. Within that canon the church reads seven primary covenants as the structural pillars of sacred history. These seven form the Zamadu, the sacred bond, between God and His people: a chain that stretches from the first breath of Adam to the outpouring of the Spirit at Pentecost. Each covenant builds upon the one before it. None is abolished; each is fulfilled, expanded, deepened.

The seven are these: the Covenant of Adam, the Covenant of Noah, the Covenant of Melchizedek, the Covenant of Abraham, the Covenant of Moses, the Covenant of David, and the New Covenant in Christ. The names are the bones of the whole biblical history, and the Ethiopian church reads them as a single constitutional line: the Sabatu Kidanat, the seven bonds, by which the Most High bound Himself to man and man to Himself.

To the Covenant of Adam belongs the creation itself, and the first promise hidden in the judgment: the seed of the woman who would bruise the serpent's head. To the Covenant of Noah belongs the sign in the cloud, the bow set as a token that the waters would no more become a flood. To the Covenant of Melchizedek belongs the eternal priesthood, the order that outranks Levi and that Christ Himself would bear. To the Covenant of Abraham belongs the call and the promise (land, seed, and blessing), sealed in the flesh of every male heir. To the Covenant of Moses belongs the law, the covenant written on stone at Sinai and carried in the Ark. To the Covenant of David belongs the throne, the kingdom sworn to stand forever, which the Ethiopian royal line claimed through the Queen of Sheba's son. And to the New Covenant in Christ belongs the fulfillment of them all: the law written on hearts, the priesthood made eternal, the throne established without end, the seed of Abraham become a blessing to every nation. ::QUOTE::He has helped his servant, Israel, in remembrance of his mercy, as he spoke to our fathers, to Abraham and his offspring forever. (Luke 1:54-55)

The chain begins in a garden, and it begins again in the same garden after the breaking. When Adam transgressed, the covenant was broken but not destroyed. God did not abandon His creation; He planted the seed of redemption in the very moment of judgment. The Ethiopian tradition teaches, through the Ethiopic Clement, that God promised Adam that He would save mankind by being born from their children after 5,500 years: a chronology that runs through the whole of Ethiopian covenant theology, binding the first Kidan to the final redemption.

The first gospel, the protoevangelium, is spoken to the serpent in the hearing of the man and the woman: ::QUOTE::I will put hostility between you and the woman, and between your offspring and her offspring. He will bruise your head, and you will bruise his heel. (Genesis 3:15)

The Book of Jubilees adds covenant detail that the Western canon does not preserve. After the expulsion from Eden, Adam offered the first sacrifice of atonement: frankincense, galbanum, stacte, and spices, offered as a sweet savour in the morning with the rising of the sun. This sacrificial act established the pattern of blood atonement that runs through every subsequent covenant, from the coats of skins to the altar of Abraham, from the tabernacle of Moses to the cross of Christ.

The Kidan Adom establishes a foundational truth that reverberates through all that follows: man was created in the Tselem, the image, of God. This is not a theological abstraction. It is a covenantal declaration of identity, purpose, and authority: a title. Every living soul bears the divine image as its title, and the Ethiopian tradition teaches that this image was not lost through the Fall but obscured, and that every subsequent covenant serves to restore the clarity of the divine image in man. ::QUOTE::God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them. (Genesis 1:27)

The breath of God, the neshamah, placed within Adam is the same breath that sustains every covenant heir. The image is the ground of the whole constitutional order: because man bears the image, man bears responsibility; because the image is obscured, the covenants exist to restore it; because the image is in Christ, the heir stands complete in the last Adam.

The seven covenants are not seven separate agreements scattered across history. They are one covenant administered in seven movements, each adding what the next needs, each fulfilled in the one who came to do the will of the Father. The Ethiopian church reads them as a constitutional whole because it reads Scripture as a constitutional whole: the Torah is the foundation, the Prophets are the commentary, the Gospels and the Apostolic writings are the administration of the covenant in the last days, and the broader canon (Enoch, Jubilees, the Meqabyan books, the Sinodos, the Didascalia, Clement) preserves the witness that the narrower canons cut away.

The Living Soul who would operate as a fiduciary steward of the Ecclesiastical Estate must know these seven covenants as a trustee knows the instruments of the trust: what was granted, to whom, under what sign, and how it is enforced. The grants run in an unbroken line from the garden to the throne. The signs are the rainbow, the circumcision, the Sabbath, the Ark, the throne of David, and the blood of the everlasting covenant. And the heir is every living soul who reads these testimonies and receives them, not as history to be admired, but as title to be claimed.

This book sets out the whole chain, volume by volume, from the primordial covenants to the covenant of the spoken word, so that the heir who reads may know what is his, and may stand upon it.
